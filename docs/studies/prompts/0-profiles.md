Lise Meitner

Biography

Lise Meitner (1878–1968) was an Austrian–Swedish physicist who earned her PhD at the University of Vienna in 1906 and joined Max Planck’s and Otto Hahn’s Berlin research group in 1907. In three decades there she pioneered studies of radioactivity, nuclear isomerism and the Auger–Meitner effect. In 1938, as a Jewish scientist, she fled Nazi Germany to Sweden and in 1939, with her nephew Otto Frisch, coined the term “fission.”

Key Contributions

- Isolation and naming of the isotope $\ce{^{231}Pa}$, advancing understanding of heavy‐element decay.  
- Discovery of the radiationless transition now known as the Auger–Meitner effect.  
- Theoretical explanation of uranium fission:  
  `math
  \ce{^{235}U + n -> ^{144}Ba + ^{89}Kr + 3 n + ~200\,MeV}
  `  
  – laid groundwork for nuclear energy and weapons research (though she opposed bomb development).

Challenges

- Systemic gender bias: often excluded from leadership roles and key publications.  
- Forced exile in 1938 due to anti-Jewish laws, leaving behind her lab and collaborators.  
- Overlooked by the Nobel Committee in 1944 despite her central role in fission’s discovery.  
- Limited resources and isolation in Swedish exile, no lab keys or technical staff.

Chien-Shiung Wu

Biography

Chien-Shiung Wu (1912–1997) was a Chinese-American experimental physicist. After earning her PhD under Ernest Lawrence at UC Berkeley in 1940, she joined the Manhattan Project at Columbia University, developing radiation-detection techniques. In 1957 she became Dupin Professor of Physics at Columbia, the first woman in that role.

Key Contributions

- Performed the “Wu Experiment” demonstrating parity violation in weak interactions by measuring $\beta$-decay of $\ce{^{60}Co}$:  
  `math
  \ce{^{60}Co -> ^{60}Ni + \beta^- + \bar\nu_e}
  `  
  – showed that mirror symmetry is not conserved.  
- Confirmed the Conservation of Vector Current in nuclear $\beta$-decay (1963).  
- Pioneered precision techniques in low-temperature detectors and nuclear spectroscopy.

Challenges

- Racial and gender discrimination: denied faculty positions early in her career.  
- Her crucial role was omitted when Lee and Yang won the 1957 Nobel Prize for parity theory.  
- Battled “first-generation immigrant” barriers while establishing herself in top laboratories.  
- Juggled family expectations with relentless experimental demands.

Barbara McClintock

Biography

Barbara McClintock (1902–1992) was an American cytogeneticist who earned her BS, MS and PhD from Cornell University (1923–1927). She spent decades at the University of Missouri and Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, carving out maize cytogenetics as her specialty and becoming one of the first to visualize individual chromosomes in action.

Key Contributions

- Genetic recombination mapping: first to link physical chromosome bands with inheritable traits.  
- Discovery of transposable elements (“jumping genes”) in maize—mobile DNA sequences (Activator “Ac” and Dissociator “Ds”) that regulate neighbouring gene expression.  
- Elucidation of the breakage–fusion–bridge cycle, explaining chromosomal instability and mutation.

Challenges

- Her “jumping gene” work was dismissed as too speculative; peers ignored it for over a decade.  
- Felt isolated—often worked without collaborators, funding or institutional support.  
- Stopped publishing in the 1950s out of frustration, awaiting later molecular confirmation.  
- Recognition only came in the 1960s–70s, culminating in the 1983 Nobel Prize.

Dorothy Hodgkin

Biography

Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin (1910–1994) was an English chemist trained at Somerville College, Oxford, and Cambridge under J.D. Bernal. In 1934 she set up her own X-ray crystallography lab at Oxford’s Natural History Museum. She became the first British woman to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1964).

Key Contributions

- Solved the 3D structure of penicillin ($\ce{C16H18N2O4S}$) in 1945, confirming its antibiotic scaffold.  
- Determined the complex corrin ring of vitamin B$_{12}$ ($\ce{C63H88CoN14O14P}$) by 1956.  
- Elucidated the structure of insulin (34 residues, two peptide chains) by 1969, transforming diabetes therapy.  
- Pioneered computational phasing methods in protein crystallography.

Challenges

- Lifelong struggle with rheumatoid arthritis, deforming hands yet persisting at the X-ray bench.  
- Juggled three children and a politically active husband during wartime and post-war demands.  
- Worked in makeshift facilities, often overshadowed by male colleagues.  
- Faced skepticism about crystallography’s applicability to large biomolecules until her success.

Gertrude B. Elion

Biography

Gertrude Belle Elion (1918–1999) was an American biochemist and pharmacologist. After graduating summa cum laude from Hunter College (1937) and earning an MS at NYU, she joined George Hitchings at Burroughs Wellcome in 1944. She pioneered structure-based drug design, earning the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Key Contributions

- 6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP) for leukemia—first antimetabolite to block DNA synthesis in cancer cells.  
- Azathioprine, the first immunosuppressant used to prevent organ-transplant rejection.  
- Acyclovir (ACV), a selective antiviral targeting herpesvirus DNA polymerase.  
- Contributed to AZT development, the first antiretroviral therapy against HIV/AIDS.

Challenges

- Weathered Depression-era financial hardship—15 graduate-fellowship rejections because she was a woman.  
- Worked as secretary and high-school teacher before landing a lab role at age 26.  
- Never earned a formal PhD; later received honorary doctorates only after international acclaim.  
- Pioneered drug-design at a time when pharmaceutical R&D was dominated by trial-and-error methods.
